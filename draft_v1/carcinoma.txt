For the sake of simplification and first draft purposes, we will assume that the carcinoma is a single well defined tumor mass with only one(Or none) necrotic core and assuming fat and scaffolding is distributed uniformly throughout the tumor
We will assume that uniform blood flow wrt to the center. That is the blood flow is only a function of the distance from the center of the tumor and distance from the edge
How is the center determined? We are taking the part that recieves the least blood flow as the center of the tumor. This is typically the area that is most hypoxic and has the highest concentration of necrotic tissue. 
The center can be identified through imaging techniques such as MRI or CT scans, which can highlight areas of low perfusion and necrosis. 
Additionally, histological analysis of biopsy samples can provide further confirmation of the tumor's center by identifying regions with the least viable cells and highest levels of necrosis.

Biopsy method:-
We didnt assume that the cells are uniformly distributed throughout the tumour, so we will take multiple biopsy samples from different regions of the tumor to ensure a representative analysis. 

How to take the biopsy? 
Options:-
- Along the chord of the tumor, taking samples at regular intervals to cover the entire mass.
- Radial sampling from the center to the periphery, ensuring that samples are taken from both the hypoxic core and the more vascularized outer regions.
- Grid sampling, where the tumor is divided into a grid pattern and samples are taken from each section to provide a comprehensive overview of the tumor

Grid sampling is more useful and more randomized but it is significanlty more challenging to implement in practice so we will ignore it for now

So we will go with Radial and Chord sampling methods for our biopsy approach. We will compare the results afterwards

What to do with the biopsy samples after analysing various cell features and putting it on proper table. Point to be noted:It is crucial that we preserve the spacial
information of the biopsy samples to maintain the context of their origin within the tumor. Also the number of cells and the volume of necrotic tissue i.e dead are not included for obvious reason

So like let's say there is a mutation A. Then if we find x number of cells with mutation A in the sample taken from a specific location of the biopsy X and 
y number of cells with mutation A in the sample taken from another specific location of the biopsy Y, we need to record these findings along with their respective spatial coordinates.
This will allow us to map the distribution of mutation A within the tumor and understand its heterogeneity.
After we have collected and analyzed the biopsy samples, we will then proceed with determining the cell population in the tumor. How to? MRI and other scans can determine the amount of scafolding,fat and other structural component. 
We remove these areas because we are concerned about the actual tumour cells. Point to be noted:Even after removing these areas, there may still be some non-tumor cells present in the samples, such as immune cells or stromal cells(MRI and other tech cant distinguish between these and tumor cells). 
We calculate the remaining volume of the mass(V).
From the biopsy samples, we remove the scafolding and fat components and count the number of cells present in each sample(Nb) and also the volume of these cells excluding scaffoding(VB)
The total number of tumour cell is basically V*(Nb/VB). This gives us an estimate of the total tumor cell population within the mass.

Now we divide the mass into countless grid. Yes, the biopsy samples also get divided into the same grid. The number of cells in each grid Vi*(Nb/VB) where Vi is the volume of grid i given that grid i is not part of the biopsy and Vi is the volume of grid i excluding the scaffolding and fat components.
If grid i is part of the biopsy, then we can directly use the number of cells counted in that grid from the biopsy sample.

Like this we get Ni = Number of cells in grid i but excludes the scaffolding and fat components. 

Similarly we can do Ni* = Number of cancer cells(The deriviation is more or less similar as before. Just exclude the non cancer cell from the count during the biopsy)

N* = Sum of Ni* over all grids i. This gives us an estimate of the total number of cancer cells in the tumor mass.
N` = Sum of Ni over all grids i. This gives us an estimate of the total number of cells in the tumor mass.

N* has other formula with V*((Nb*)/VB) and N` has other formula with V*((Nb)/VB). I am not sure which of them is better but we can compare the results(N* vs V*((Nb*)/VB) and N` vs V*((Nb)/VB)) of both mathamatically. This is important


Interpolation:-
Grids closer to each other have more similar cell populations than grids that are farther apart. Therefore, we can use interpolation techniques to estimate the number of cells in grids that were not directly sampled during the biopsy.
We can apply distanced-based interpolation methods, such as inverse distance weighting or kriging, or even more complex bayesian model or ml model but that is for later. For now we will assume that the method is a blackbox and it gives us the entire tumour population distribution


Modeling population:-We will assume outmigration and immigration of cells is negligible because it is actually negligible in context of solid mass

So N* = Innitial population of cancer cells in the tumor mass + B* - D* where B* is the total number of cancer cells born and D* is the total number of cancer cells that died.

Ni* = Innitial population of cancer cells in grid i + Bi* - Di* where Bi* is the number of cancer cells born in grid i and Di* is the number of cancer cells that died in grid i.

There are already methodology on how to determine the birth and death rates of cancer cells based on the various factors using the biopsy samples

But now we have to integrate the effect of a treatment which slows down or speeds up the birth and death rates of the cancer cells. This can be done by analyzing the properties of the treatment and simulation

But the amount of treatment that reaches each grid will depend on the blood flow to that grid and other factors 

So we will model it as follows:-

Ni* = Innitial population of cancer cells in grid i + Bi* - Di* + BTi* - DTi* where BTi* is the number of cancer cells born in grid i due to treatment(Maybe negative if it stops the birth rate) and DTi* is the number of cancer cells that died in grid i due to treatment.

Accounting for time and writing everything properly we have

Ni*(t+1) = Ni*(t) + Bi*(t,Ni*(t)) - Di*(t,Ni*(t)) + BTi*(t,Ni*(t)) - DTi*(t,Ni*(t))

And with this you can simulate the dynamics of the cancer cell population in each grid over time, taking into account the effects of treatment and the inherent birth and death rates of the cells.


Let A be some sort of cell mutation or something like that. Then we have

Even more importantly we can have Ni*(t+1, Ni*(t), A) = Ni*(t, Ni*(t-1), A) + Bi*(t, Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni*(t-1), A), A) - Di*(t, Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni*(t-1), A), A) + BTi*(t, Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni*(t-1), A), A) - DTi*(t, Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni*(t-1), A), A). The notation have usual meaning that is Ni(...,A) means only the cell with mutation A and so on

Why Ni*(t) matter above? Because there is a limit to the number of cells due to resources and stuff. Will it also depend of N^(t)? Actually yes. IT should rely on N^(t) and N*(t) both and that is what we have done in the following equations

Let N^(t) = N'(t) - N*(t). and Ni^(t) = Ni'(t) - Ni*(t) where N^(t) is the total number of non-cancer cells in the tumor mass at time t and Ni^(t) is the total number of non-cancer cells in grid i at time t. 

Ni^(t+1) = Ni^(t) + Bi^(t,Ni^(t),Ni*(t)) - Di^(t,Ni^(t),Ni*(t)) + BTi^(t,Ni^(t),Ni*(t)) - DTi^(t,Ni^(t),Ni*(t)). The effect of drug on non cancer cell is pretty well established in literature so we can easily model it. No extra info needed

N^(t) = Sum of Ni^(t) over all grids i. This gives us an estimate of the total number of non-cancer cells in the tumor mass at time t.

Similarly we get the following

Ni*(t+1) = Ni*(t) + Bi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t)) - Di*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t)) + BTi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t)) - DTi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t))

Ni*(t+1, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), A) = Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A) + Bi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A), A) - Di*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A), A) + BTi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A), A) - DTi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A), A)



Let Ii(t) = (Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A1), Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A2), ..., Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), An)) be the vector of cancer cell populations with different combitorial combo of mutations at time t. 

Updated equations for the cancer cell populations with different mutations can be written as:

Ni*(t+1, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), A) = Ni*(t, Ni^(t-1), Ni*(t-1), A) + Bi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ii(t), A) - Di*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ii(t), A) + BTi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ii(t), A) - DTi*(t, Ni^(t), Ni*(t), Ii(t), A)

Why? Because one mutation can transition to another mutation

Note:- If A, B are 2 diffrent mutation then I includes just A, just B, both A and B or none of these mutation(Catch box for other mutation). So if we have 2 mutations then we have 4 different types of cells. This can overshoot really fast so best is that we consider the most dominant ones

Cell to volume:-

Vi_total(t) = Ni*(t) * (Volume of individual cancer cell+Necessary packing) + Ni^(t) * (Volume of individual healthy cell+Necessary packing) + Ri(t) + Si(t) + Ei(t)

Ri(t) = Volume of dead cell debris in grid i at time t
Si(t) = Volume of scaffolding and fat components in grid i at time t
Ei(t) = Due to fluid accumulation and other stuff in grid i at time t

NOTE:Under drug/treatment, the volume of cell is not constant. It can change due to various factors such as cell swelling, shrinkage, or changes in the extracellular matrix.
